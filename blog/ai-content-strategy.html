How AI is changing content strategy (and what still needs a human).

AI can write fast, but it can't decide what's worth saying. A content specialist's take on building an AI content strategy that still sounds like you.

Maria PeFreelance Web & Content Consultant · October 2026 · 4 min read

  2. What AI has actually changed

I run a content department of one

In my day job, I'm the only person in a content department. I lead the content system, which means AI agents do a lot of the drafting, researching and checking, and I make the calls. So when people ask me how AI is changing content strategy, I'm not guessing. I live it every week.

Here's what I've learned. AI has changed how fast we can make content, and it's changed where people find it. What it hasn't changed is the part that decides whether content works: knowing what's worth saying, to whom, and why you're the right person to say it.

AI is a powerful tool. It isn't the expert. How you train the system decides what comes out of it.

What AI has actually changed

Three things have really shifted: how much content anyone can produce, how fast, and where people go to find answers.

Volume is cheap now. Anyone can publish ten blog posts a day. That sounds like an advantage until you realise everyone else can too. The web is filling up with content that says the same things in slightly different words. Google has a name for it: "commodity content," the kind of thing "based on common knowledge, which could originate from anyone" (Google Search Central).

Speed has changed my day. Research that used to take me [your real before time] now takes [your real after time]. First drafts, outlines and QA checks happen in minutes. That time doesn't disappear. I spend it on strategy, editing and the parts only a person can do.

People search differently. Customers now ask ChatGPT, Perplexity or Google's AI Overviews and get a summary with a few sources named. Your content isn't only competing for a click anymore. It's competing to be one of the sources the AI trusts enough to quote. I wrote more about that in how to rank in AI search.

What AI hasn't changed

The hard parts of content strategy are still human: deciding what to say, sounding like yourself, and having something real to share.

Strategy. AI can write a post about anything. It can't tell you which post your business needs, who it's for, or what you want that reader to do next. That comes from knowing the business, the market and the customer.

Voice. Left alone, AI writes in the same smooth, slightly empty tone. You've read it a hundred times. Readers can spot it, and it makes a brand forgettable. Every draft I put out gets a human pass. I cut the stock phrases, add a detail only the business would know, and read it out loud.

Expertise. This is the big one. Google's own advice is to share "a unique point of view" and first-hand experience, and not to "recycle what others on the internet have already said, or could easily be produced by a generative AI model." AI can't have your experience for you. Only you have your projects, your results and your opinions.

Judgement. AI gets things wrong, and it says them confidently. Someone still has to check the facts, catch the claims that aren't true, and decide what's safe to publish.

How I build an AI content strategy that works now

A good AI content strategy uses AI for speed and people for substance. This is the process I follow, for my own work and for clients.

  1. Start with the business goal, not the tool. What should this content do? Bring in leads, answer pre-sale questions, build trust? Write that down before anyone opens ChatGPT.
  2. Find what you know that others don't. List your real projects, numbers, customer questions and opinions. This is your raw material, and it's what makes content worth quoting.
  3. Check the search data. I use Semrush to see what people actually search for and how they phrase it. Then I check what shows up in Google and the AI answers for those searches.
  4. Let AI do the heavy lifting. Research summaries, outlines, first drafts, QA checklists. This is where AI saves real hours.
  5. Train the system on your voice. A short voice guide and a few good examples change the output a lot. This is the part most people skip, and it's why their AI content all sounds the same.
  6. Edit like a person. Add your experience, cut the filler, check every fact. If you can't back a claim up, take it out.
  7. Publish less, but better. One strong article with real insight beats ten that repeat what's already out there.
  8. Measure what matters. Track leads and enquiries, not just traffic. Look at visits from AI tools too, like chatgpt.com and perplexity.ai.

FAQ

Does Google penalise AI-written content?+

Not for being written with AI. Google's guidance says if you use AI tools, the content still has to meet its quality standards and spam policies. Mass-producing pages to game search is what gets you in trouble.

Can AI replace a content writer?+

It can replace some of the typing. It can't replace the strategy, the voice, or the expertise. In my experience, the best results come from a skilled person running a well-trained AI system.

Where should a small business start?+

Pick one goal, write down what you know that your competitors don't, and use AI to help you turn that into one really good piece. Then build from there.
